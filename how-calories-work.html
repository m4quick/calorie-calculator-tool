How Calorie Needs Are Calculated

Every calorie target starts from the same two questions: how much energy does your body use just staying alive, and how much more does it use doing everything else? Answer both and you have your maintenance level — the number every other goal is set relative to.

Step 1 — BMR, the cost of existing

Your Basal Metabolic Rate is the energy your body burns at complete rest: breathing, circulating blood, maintaining temperature, repairing tissue. It is the largest single component of most people's daily burn, typically around 60–70% of the total, and it is not something you consciously control.

Our calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which is generally regarded as the most accurate of the widely used BMR formulas for the general population:

BMR — Mifflin-St Jeor

Men: (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) + 5

Women: (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Notice what the equation needs: weight, height, age and sex. All four genuinely change the answer, which is why a calculator that omits any of them is guessing. Height matters because a taller body has more tissue to maintain; age matters because resting metabolism declines gradually over adulthood.

Step 2 — Activity multiplier, the cost of living

BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor to give TDEE — Total Daily Energy Expenditure — your actual maintenance calories:

LevelMultiplierRoughly means
Sedentary1.2Desk job, little deliberate exercise
Light1.375Light exercise 1–3 days a week
Moderate1.55Exercise 3–5 days a week
Active1.725Hard exercise 6–7 days a week
Very active1.9Physical job or twice-daily training

This step is where most of the error creeps in. The multipliers are broad bands, and people reliably overestimate their own activity — a few gym sessions a week alongside a desk job is usually "light," not "active." If your results consistently miss, the activity level is the first input to revisit, not the formula.

Step 3 — Adjusting for a goal

Once you know maintenance, a goal is just an adjustment:

  • Weight loss: subtract about 500 calories a day for roughly 1 lb per week, working from the approximation that 3,500 calories equals about a pound of fat.
  • Faster loss: subtracting 1,000 a day targets about 2 lb per week, but that size of deficit is harder to sustain and increases muscle loss.
  • Maintenance: eat at TDEE.
  • Gain: add 250–500 a day, paired with resistance training so the surplus builds muscle rather than only fat.

Why the 3,500-calorie rule overpromises

It treats weight loss as linear, and it is not. As you lose weight your BMR falls — a smaller body costs less to run — so the same intake that produced a deficit in month one produces a smaller one by month four. This is the ordinary reason for a plateau, and the fix is usually to recalculate at your current weight rather than to cut harder.

Understanding your results

  • BMR — calories burned at complete rest. Never a target to eat at; it is a reference point.
  • TDEE — your maintenance level, including all activity and digestion.
  • Target — TDEE adjusted for your goal.
  • Minimum — the floor below which intake is not generally advisable without medical supervision. Commonly cited as about 1,200 calories for women and 1,500 for men.

How accurate is any of this?

Treat the output as a well-informed starting point, not a measurement. Even the best formula estimates BMR within roughly 10% for most people, and further off for those at the extremes of body composition — a very muscular person's needs are typically underestimated, since the equation cannot distinguish muscle from fat.

The practical approach is to use the number as a hypothesis: eat at it consistently for two to three weeks, track what actually happens to your weight, and adjust from the observed result. Your own trend line beats any equation.
